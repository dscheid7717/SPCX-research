## What P/E is, and why use it alongside a DCF

**What the two inputs measure.** Price per share is what the market currently pays to own one
share. Diluted earnings per share is the annual accounting profit attributable to one share,
after allowing for shares that could be issued under outstanding equity awards. Dividing one by
the other gives **dollars of share price per dollar of annual earnings** — a multiple, not a
dollar amount. Read loosely, a 10× multiple means the price equals about ten years of current
earnings, holding earnings flat.

**Why the multiple, not the price.** Share price alone compares nothing, because the number of
shares a company has outstanding is largely an accident of its history. Group 1 trades at
$421.48 and AutoNation at $169.84, but that gap says nothing about which is more expensive.
Dividing by EPS removes share count from the comparison and puts companies of very different
size on a common footing.

**What it adds to a DCF.** A discounted cash flow values a company from *my* forecast of its
future cash flows — it is an internal estimate, and its answer is only as good as my
assumptions. A comparables analysis asks a different question: what would this company be worth
if investors priced its earnings the way they price similar companies' earnings? That is
**external market evidence**, sourced from prices other people are actually paying.

The two are independent checks, which is the point of running both. When they disagree, the
disagreement is a prompt to inspect the peer set, the earnings definition, and the forecast
assumptions — not a reason to average the two answers into a false consensus. Neither number
proves the other wrong.

**When it is useful — and when it misleads.** P/E works when positive earnings reasonably
represent the business and peers share comparable economics. The comparison breaks down when:

- **Earnings are negative or zero.** A loss divided into a positive price gives a negative
  number that is not a meaningful multiple. This is a limitation to name, not to fix by
  switching to adjusted earnings.
- **Earnings are unusual.** A one-time gain inflates the denominator and makes the multiple look
  deceptively cheap; a temporarily depressed year makes it look expensive. The multiple is only
  as representative as the earnings underneath it.
- **The businesses genuinely differ.** Different growth prospects, risk, or leverage justify
  genuinely different multiples. A lower P/E may be correctly lower.

**What must match** before two multiples are comparable: the business model, the earnings
definition (reported versus adjusted), the earnings period, the currency and share basis, the
stock-split basis, and the pricing date. This comparison holds all of those constant — total
GAAP diluted EPS for the year ended December 31, 2024, priced at the December 31, 2024 close.

## The peer policy, stated before the numbers

A candidate is a company worth investigating. A peer is a candidate whose economics I can
defend under a stated policy. Sharing an industry label does not settle that judgment, so I
fixed the tests before looking at any multiple:

1. **Business model** — a publicly traded franchised vehicle retailer selling new and used
   vehicles, with meaningful parts and service operations.
2. **Economics** — comparable in how the company earns money, its geography, and its financing
   activities. Material differences get named, not buried in an average.
3. **Earnings** — positive annual earnings on a consistent definition. I use **total GAAP
   diluted EPS** throughout, not management's adjusted figures.
4. **Disposition** — qualify a candidate carrying a material business difference; exclude one
   that does not fit the business model, or whose evidence cannot be verified.

Both candidates clear the earnings test: Asbury at $21.50, AutoNation at $16.92, and Group 1 at
$36.81 are all positive, so a P/E comparison is meaningful for each. That is not automatic, and
it is the first condition that would have ended this exercise had it failed.

## AutoNation (AN) — **Use**

AutoNation matches Asbury across the entire revenue engine: new and used vehicle retail,
recurring parts and service income, and finance and insurance products attached at the point of
sale. On the core question of how the company makes money, the two are the same business.

The one material difference is **AutoNation Finance**, a captive lending operation. Asbury
primarily *arranges* financing through third-party lenders and earns commission on the
placement; AutoNation *originates and holds* loans on its own balance sheet. That puts interest
income and credit-loss provisions into AutoNation's earnings that Asbury's income statement
does not carry, and it makes consumer credit performance a distinct earnings driver.

I record that difference rather than averaging it away, but it does not change what kind of
company AutoNation is. Captive finance is one segment layered onto an otherwise matching model,
and it does not break the earnings comparison. **AutoNation is used as a peer without
qualification.**

## Group 1 Automotive (GPI) — **Qualify**

Group 1 runs the same business model as Asbury — franchised vehicle retail, parts and service,
and finance and insurance. It fails none of the model tests. Two material differences keep it
from being a clean comparison, and they compound each other.

**Operating region.** Group 1 operates in both the United States and the United Kingdom, while
Asbury is domestic. That brings a second market with its own margin structure, competitive
dynamics, regulatory environment, and currency translation. The consolidated earnings being
compared are not the earnings of a single national business.

**Acquisition scale and timing.** Group 1 acquired **54 Inchcape dealerships during 2024**. Its
FY2024 earnings therefore include only a partial-year contribution from those stores, alongside
whatever costs the transaction carried. That matters specifically for a P/E: the December 31
share price reflects the *full* acquired business, while the EPS denominator captures only part
of its first year. The two sides of the ratio are measuring different versions of the company.

Neither difference is grounds for exclusion. Both are disclosed, verifiable, and sit within the
same business model — which is what a qualification is for. **Group 1 is retained as a
qualified peer.**

### What the qualification obligates me to do

Qualifying a peer means keeping the difference visible and testing its consequence. It is not a
hidden discount applied to the answer.

Group 1 carries the higher multiple of the two, so it pulls the peer-implied price upward. The
leave-one-peer-out calculation is the test of that qualification: it shows exactly how much of
the implied price depends on the peer I flagged as imperfect. That is the mechanism by which
the peer judgment above connects to the numbers below, rather than sitting beside them as
commentary.

## An earnings-definition check worth recording

Group 1's own earnings release leads with **continuing operations**. This comparison uses
**total** diluted EPS of $36.81, matching the basis used for Asbury and AutoNation.

Substituting the continuing-operations figure would change Group 1's multiple and every number
derived from it, while still looking like a defensible choice. Reading the row label rather
than the first earnings number on the page is the control that prevents it. I have not
substituted adjusted EPS anywhere in this comparison.

## Prediction — written before running the leave-one-out

**Removing Group 1 will lower the peer multiple, and therefore lower the implied price for
Asbury.** Group 1's multiple is the higher of the two, so dropping it leaves the lower
AutoNation multiple as the only observation and pulls the estimate down.

## The changed-peer result

| | Estimate | Change from $231.00 |
|---|---:|---:|
| Both peers (median) | $231.00 | — |
| Remove AutoNation → GPI alone | $246.18 | **+$15.18** |
| **Remove Group 1 → AN alone** | **$215.81** | **−$15.18** |

**The prediction holds.** Removing Group 1 — the higher-multiple peer — lowers the estimate, by
$15.18 a share. The symmetry is not a coincidence: with exactly two observations the median sits
at the midpoint, so removing either peer moves the estimate to the other one's multiple, the
same distance in opposite directions.

**Why this is now a reference estimate and not a range.** A range requires at least two
observations to bracket. With Group 1 removed, AutoNation is the only remaining evidence, and
one number cannot bracket anything — the minimum, median, and maximum all collapse onto the
same $215.81. The calculator labels it a reference estimate for that reason. Losing the range is
a real loss of information, not a cosmetic change in wording.

**What this means for the qualification.** About 6.6% of the two-peer estimate ($15.18 of
$231.00) rests on the peer I flagged as imperfect. That is the consequence of the Group 1
qualification made visible and measured, which is what qualifying a peer is supposed to produce.
I keep Group 1 in the comparison — its business model genuinely matches, and the removal test is
run to show what depends on it, not to justify dropping it because the resulting price is lower.

## What this comparison does and does not establish

**What P/E measures.** Price per share divided by annual earnings per share: dollars of share
price for each dollar of annual earnings. Dividing by EPS is what makes companies of different
size comparable, since it removes the effect of how many shares a company happens to have
outstanding.

**Why a lower multiple is not automatically a better buy.** A lower P/E is a question, not a
verdict. It can reflect slower expected growth, higher risk, more leverage, or earnings that are
temporarily inflated — an unusual one-time profit makes the denominator large and the multiple
look deceptively cheap. AutoNation's lower multiple is a prompt to ask why the market prices its
earnings less richly than Group 1's, not evidence that it is the better investment.

**Why this does not prove Asbury is fairly valued.** Asbury's observed price of $243.03 does sit
inside the $215.81–$246.18 band — near the top of it. That establishes nothing about fairness.
The band is entirely a product of two peers and one earnings convention: change the peer set,
switch to adjusted EPS, or use a different date, and the band moves. A two-peer comparison is a
small piece of market evidence, not a valuation conclusion, and it says nothing about whether
the underlying business is worth owning.

**No cash or debt bridge.** P/E is an equity multiple — price per share is already what is left
for shareholders after lenders are paid — so these implied prices are final. Adding cash or
subtracting debt would double-count the capital structure. That bridge belongs to
enterprise-value methods such as the FCFF discounted cash flow built in Lab 06, where the cash
flow being discounted is pre-financing and therefore belongs to lenders and shareholders
together. The calculator prints this note on every run.
