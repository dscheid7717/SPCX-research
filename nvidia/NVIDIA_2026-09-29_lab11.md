## I — the sensitivity rig

[`nvidia/sensitivity.py`](sensitivity.py), standard library only, importing the Lab 10 engine
rather than copying it.

```
cd nvidia && python sensitivity.py              # the full table
cd nvidia && python sensitivity.py --selftest   # prove the rig before trusting it
cd nvidia && python sensitivity.py --trace "Gross margin higher"   # statements for one run
```

### Why a snapshot, not save-and-restore

The Lab 09 sensitivity on Asbury saved the one constant it was about to change and put it back in
a `finally` block. That is enough when every input is a float. Here **four inputs are lists** —
the revenue growth, gross margin, R&D and SG&A paths — and a list handed to the engine can be
mutated in place, which would leak into every later run and never announce itself.

So this file snapshots **all 24 independent inputs** with a deep copy at import, and restores all
24 before every single run. A run cannot inherit anything from the run before it, whether or not
the previous run raised. Calculated statement totals are never overridden — they have to
recalculate, or the test is not testing a linked model.

### The rig proves itself before it is trusted

`--selftest` exercises the machinery only, so it can be run before the drivers are chosen and
**cannot reveal a sensitivity result**:

```
Three base runs identical                58.226350   OK
Override with base value is a no-op      58.226350   OK
Base survives a refused run              58.226350   OK
List input restored after override  [0.3, 0.22, 0.16, 0.11, 0.07]   OK
SELF-CHECK PASSED
```

| Check | The failure it catches |
|---|---|
| Three base runs identical | state leaking between runs |
| Override with the base value is a no-op | the override path differing from the no-override path |
| Base survives a refused run | a raised exception leaving a changed input behind |
| List input restored after override | a five-year path mutated in place |

The third deserves a note. Setting terminal growth above the cost of equity makes the Gordon
formula undefined and the engine refuses — and base still reproduces to twelve decimals
afterwards. **A refusal is a result, not a crash.** Invalid runs are flagged and excluded from the
spans rather than ranked.

### Tracing one result by hand

Gross margin's effect on final-year operating profit is reproducible in closed form, without
running anything. A +2pp margin adds 2pp of revenue to gross profit, and SG&A takes 3% of that
back because it is set as a ratio *to gross profit*:

```
0.02 x 471,842.44 x (1 - 0.03) = 9,153.74
```

The model reports **9,153.74**. That match is the strongest evidence in this lab that the run
changed only what I said it changed — an independent input moved, and the linked lines
recalculated exactly as the assumption table says they should.

Revenue growth does not reduce to a one-liner, because it compounds. The +2pp path lifts FY2031
revenue from 471,842 to 513,702, and the +23,050 of operating profit on that +41,860 of revenue is
a **55.1% incremental margin** — gross margin of 69%, less R&D at 10.5% of revenue, less SG&A at
about 2.1% of revenue. Growth is not free in this model: two of the three largest cost lines scale
with it.

First, a percentage point of margin is a percentage point of *revenue*, and by FY2031 revenue is

Second, **59.0% of this valuation sits in the terminal value**, which capitalises final-year FCFE.
Anything that moves the final year moves the majority of the answer with it. That amplifies *both*
drivers, and I had not carried it into either estimate.

**The part of my reasoning that could not have worked.** I predicted that strategic capital —

revenue-growth effect. It cannot, in this model: Lab 10 holds the 22,251 of non-marketable equity
securities flat and explicitly excludes the 9,022 of other income, 8,918 of it investment gains. I
flagged this in the locked record before running. The ranking I predicted was right, but one of
the two reasons I gave for it is not a mechanism this model contains. It remains a real-world
reason the market might react more sharply than my model does, and that belongs in the
interpretation rather than in the mechanism.

**Revenue growth is the larger driver on all three outputs, over these ranges.**

The qualifier is not a formality. Both ranges are ±2 percentage points, chosen to be equal
precisely so the ranking would mean something — but equal *point* shifts are not equal *economic*
uncertainty. NVIDIA's revenue growth has historically swung far more than its margin: growth moved
48.7 points year over year (114.2% to 65.5%) while margin moved 3.9 points across three years.
Sized to real-world uncertainty rather than to equal points, revenue growth's lead would widen,
not narrow. The ranking here is a floor on its dominance, not a ceiling.

### The result that surprised me: growth is convex, margin is linear

Gross margin's changes from base are **exactly symmetric**: −9,153.74 and +9,153.74 on operating
profit, −2.11 and +2.11 on value. Revenue growth's are not: **−3.72 against +3.94** on value, an
asymmetry of $0.22.

That is not rounding. Margin is a level applied once to a revenue base that growth has already
fixed, so its effect is linear. Growth feeds itself — each year's rate applies to the revenue the
previous year produced — so five years of +2pp compound into more than five years of −2pp take
away. **Revenue growth is the only input in this model that is an argument of its own function.**
Convexity is the mathematical shape of that.

It also means a symmetric input range produces an asymmetric output range for growth, and any
reading of the span as "value is equally likely to be $54.51 or $62.17" is wrong twice over — once
because the endpoints are not equally likely, and once because they are not equally far from base.

### Why my company's driver may differ from Colin's

Ranking unlike companies by raw dollar change would be meaningless — NVIDIA's revenue is roughly
half a trillion dollars by FY2031, so every span here is large in absolute terms. The comparable
question is structural: which driver leads, and why. NVIDIA is a high-growth, high-margin business
whose costs scale with revenue, so growth compounds and margin does not. A mature company with a
fixed cost base would show the opposite — margin dropping straight to operating profit with
nothing scaling to offset it, and growth adding little because the growth rates themselves are
small.

Colin's Microsoft model showed the same convexity in revenue growth, with an even more
pronounced upside-versus-downside asymmetry. See the exchange notes below, including why his
$14.20 per percentage point and my $1.92 per percentage point cannot be compared until both are
expressed as a share of their own base value per share.

## Sensitivity — learn on your own

**1. What is one-at-a-time sensitivity?** Changing exactly one independent input, holding every
other independent input at base, rebuilding the whole linked model, and recording what moved.
Repeat per input. It measures *response* — how much an output moves per unit of input — and its
defining limitation is that it captures no interactions. Moving growth and margin together is not
the sum of moving each alone, because in this model margin is applied to the revenue that growth
produced. One-at-a-time is the right tool for **ranking** drivers; it is the wrong tool for
building scenarios.

**2. How does the chosen input range affect the ranking?** Completely, and this is the trap. A
span is measured in output units across the range *I* chose, so a driver with a generous range
will out-span a driver with a tight one even if the tight-range driver has far more leverage per
unit. The ABG worked example makes the point cleanly: halving the gross-margin range while leaving
capital spending alone flipped the cash-flow and value ranking, and nothing about the company
changed. That is why every conclusion has to be stated **"over these ranges"** — the ranking is a
joint fact about the model *and* my range choices. I set both ranges to equal ±2pp shifts
specifically so the comparison would be between drivers rather than between my own generosity.

**3. Why a sensitivity table is not a forecast probability.** The table says what the output would
be *if* an input took a value. It says nothing about how likely that value is. Nothing in the
calculation carries a distribution, a confidence level, or a weight — the lower and higher values
are endpoints I selected, not percentiles of anything. So the $54.51–$62.17 growth span is not a
range NVIDIA's value is 90% likely to fall in, and the endpoints are not equally likely, or even
equally distant from base, as the convexity above shows. Sensitivity measures the **response**;
uncertainty is about how well I know the input, and these are different questions. Reading a span
as a probability would also understate the downside, because a one-at-a-time table holds
everything else fixed — and in a real downturn growth and margin fall together.

## Reflect

**Which driver mattered most over my ranges:** revenue growth, on all three outputs, at 1.8× gross
margin's span for value and 2.4× for operating profit. My prediction of the ranking was right and
my sizing of growth was right; my sizing of margin was not.

**Which result surprised me:** that gross margin moved value nearly twice as much as I predicted —
1.81% per percentage point against a ceiling of 1%. The lesson generalises past this lab. I was
sizing margin against *margin*, when what matters is that a percentage point of margin is a
percentage point of a $472B revenue base, amplified by a terminal value that is 59% of the answer.
Anything that touches the final year touches most of the valuation. That is a habit of thought to
carry, not a fact about NVIDIA.
