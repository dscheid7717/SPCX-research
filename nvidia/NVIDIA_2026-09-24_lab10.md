### Organic growth — what NVIDIA discloses

**It discloses none.** NVIDIA reports no same-store, organic, or comparable-growth measure; its
MD&A discusses revenue by reportable segment (Compute & Networking 193,479, Graphics 22,459)
and by geography, not by owned-versus-acquired. So the ABG lesson — reported growth can be
bought, carry the growth of the stores already owned — cannot be applied by reading it off a
disclosure here.

It still bites. FY2026 was an acquisition year: goodwill rose from 5,188 to 20,832, cash
acquisitions were 1,535, accrued purchase consideration jumped from 9 to 3,921, and NVIDIA paid
13,000 for Groq. **NVIDIA does not disclose the revenue those acquisitions contributed**, so the
organic share of the 65.5% reported growth is `unresolved`. I have not adjusted for it and I am
not going to estimate it. What I can say is the direction: the organic figure is at or below
65.5%, never above, so a growth path anchored on the reported number is anchored slightly high.

**Why the video carries 1.8% for ABG when reported growth was 4.7%:** because a dealer buys
stores. Revenue arrives with the acquisition whether or not the business got any better, and
paying for it again in the terminal value would double-count the purchase price. 1.8% is what
the stores ABG already owned actually did.

## The line that makes NVIDIA different

> **ABG's floor plan is a lender paying for the cars on the lot. NVIDIA's equivalent is
> `excess inventory purchase obligations` — and it runs the other way.**

2,739 at FY2026, against 2,095 at FY2025, inside accrued and other current liabilities. NVIDIA
owns no fabs, so it must commit to foundry and memory capacity years before it knows what it can
sell. When it commits to more than it can sell, the accrued loss lands here.

| | floor plan (ABG) | supply obligations (NVDA) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | a loan against inventory on the lot | an accrual for inventory already committed to and not wanted |
| What it says when it grows | the lender is funding the business | the company over-ordered |
| Effect on cash | inventory build is financed, cash unharmed | expense accrued but not yet paid, so cash unharmed |

**Mechanically the two behave identically** — both scale with inventory, and an increase in
either adds back to cash because the expense was accrued rather than paid. So it is modelled
exactly the way floor plan was: as a ratio to inventory, with the change routed through FCFE,
and treated the same way in the balance sheet, the cash flow and the valuation.

**Economically they point in opposite directions**, and that is the sentence I would defend. For
a dealer, financed inventory is a sign the model is working. For a fabless designer, a growing
over-commitment accrual is a warning — it is the price of guessing wrong about demand two years
out. In this model it grows from 2,739 to 6,413 by FY2031 because inventory grows; if the ratio
itself started climbing, that would be the first evidence the AI build-out had turned.

## I — the implementation

[`nvidia/proforma.py`](proforma.py), standard library only. Run with `cd nvidia && python
proforma.py`.

The order of operations is the design: income statement, then every balance-sheet line **except**
cash, then free cash flow to equity, and only then cash. Three conventions worth naming:

- **Depreciation and interest are charged on opening balances**, both directions. Assets bought
  during the year were not in service all year, and money borrowed during the year was not
  outstanding for all of it.
- **Interest income is modelled explicitly.** NVIDIA earns 2,300 and pays 259. Leaving income
  out — as ABG's engine does, because a dealer has none — would understate pretax income by
  about 2,700 in the first year.
- **Stock-based compensation is booked twice, and it must be.** Added back in the cash flow
  because it moved no cash, *and* credited to equity because it is paid in shares. This is the
  one that bites: do only the first and the balance sheet is off by exactly the SBC amount, every
  year. It is the error the check block is best at catching, and it is demonstrated under V.

### The opening balance sheet

Mapped from the filed FY2026 sheet into the engine's buckets. Two mappings are deliberate:

- **cash = 62,556** — cash and equivalents 10,605 *plus* marketable securities 51,951. NVIDIA
  runs one liquid treasury and reports interest income on the whole of it; splitting them would
  put the earning asset in one bucket and its earnings in another. This is the same 62,556
  carried as non-operating cash in the Lab 06 DCF.
- **other assets = 112,461** — receivables, goodwill, intangibles, deferred tax assets,
  non-marketable equity securities, operating lease assets and prepaids, derived by subtraction.

Because those two lines are derived, they cannot disagree with the engine. They *can* disagree
with the 10-K, so `check_opening_balance()` runs before anything is projected and reconciles to
the filed totals:

```
Assets, mapped into the engine                     206,803.0
Assets, as filed                                   206,803.0
Liabilities, mapped into the engine                 49,510.0
Liabilities, as filed                               49,510.0
Assets - liabilities - equity                            0.0
```

### The correction — and it changed the answer

**My first version was wrong, and the check block did not catch it**, because it was not a
balancing error. It balanced perfectly and was still wrong.

In that version the buyback and dividend were the only distributions, so surplus cash stayed on
the balance sheet. It compounded to **831,139** by FY2031 and threw off **27,337** of interest
income — income the model then added to FCFE and discounted. But discounting FCFE *assumes every
dollar of it reaches shareholders in the year it is earned*. So the model was earning interest on
cash the valuation had already treated as paid out. The same dollars, counted twice.

The fix: hold the treasury flat at 62,556 and return everything above it as a residual **surplus
distribution**. Value per share fell from **$62.43 to $58.23**, about a tenth.

| | first version | corrected |
|---|---:|---:|
| FY2031E cash | 831,139 | 62,556 |
| FY2031E interest income | 27,337 | 2,721 |
| FY2031E FCFE | 243,600 | 222,922 |
| Value per share | $62.43 | $58.23 |

**The surplus distribution is large and NVIDIA is not actually paying it.** It runs 96,193 in
FY2027E rising to 181,922 in FY2031E, against a real buyback of 40,086 and a real dividend of
974. NVIDIA is accumulating, not distributing. That is a genuine disagreement between my model
and the company's behaviour, not an arithmetic error, and the honest reading is that a DCF of
this shape **cannot** value a hoarding company without either assuming distribution or explicitly
penalising the hoard. I have assumed distribution and am flagging it rather than smoothing it
away.

### The model refuses

`python proforma.py --prove-refusal` injects the realistic error — SBC added back in cash flow
but never credited to equity — and the model stops:

```
Pro-forma checks failed -- refusing to value these statements.
  FY2027E: balance sheet does not balance. Assets - liabilities - equity = 8,301.8
  ...
The FY2027E gap of 8,301.8 is exactly that year's stock-based
compensation of 8,301.8. The check found the real error.
```

The gap equals the SBC exactly, which is how I know the check is reading a real error rather
than float noise. Exit code 1; no valuation printed.

### The model against the market — a question, not a call

> The model says **$58.23**. The market says **$224.58** on **September 24, 2026**, on the same
> 24,514 million diluted shares. The model is at **0.26×** the market — the same ratio Lab 06
> reached independently.

Two reverse solves, because the direction of the error matters more than its size:

1. **Hold my forecasts, solve for the discount rate.** The market price implies a cost of equity
   of **6.54%** — a beta of **0.32**. NVIDIA's own five-year monthly return history says its beta
   is **2.216**, and its annualized volatility is 49.98% against the index's 15.70%. The market
   is not pricing NVDA like a 0.32-beta stock in any other respect.
2. **Hold my discount rate, solve for growth.** Justifying $224.58 at 16.03% needs **62.96%
   revenue growth every year for five years** — FY2031 revenue of **2,481,600**, which is
   **11.5×** FY2026.

Both cannot be true, and I do not think either is. The honest statement is that my base case and
the market disagree by a factor of nearly four, and that at least one of three things must be
wrong: the beta, the growth path, or the market. That is the question I would take into the next
lab — **not** a recommendation, and it does not by itself move the WATCH–DEFER call carried from
Lab 08.

## E — fresh eyes

**Partner:** Eleanor · **Date:** September 24, 2026

### Her attack on my table

> *"The R&D increase seems unreasonable."*

Aimed at R&D rising from 8.5% to 10.5% of revenue across FY2027E–FY2031E — the one assumption in
my table that moves **against** the trend in the history. R&D fell 14.24% → 9.90% → 8.57% over
FY2024–FY2026, and I am forecasting it back up. She is entitled to ask why I get to reverse a
ratio that fell for three straight years.

### My answer

The ratio fell for a reason that expires. R&D did not shrink — it more than doubled in dollars,
from 8,675 to 18,497. It fell as a *percentage* only because revenue tripled underneath it, and
my own growth path says that stops: revenue decelerates from 30% to 7% by FY2031, so the
denominator stops doing the work. A research budget committed years in advance is the last thing
a company in a silicon roadmap race cuts, so holding 8.57% flat would quietly assume NVIDIA can
throttle its pipeline to match its revenue line. That is the assumption I think is unreasonable,
not mine.

**What would change my mind:** two consecutive quarters in which R&D dollars grow more slowly
than revenue while revenue growth is already below 20%. That would be evidence NVIDIA actually
can flex the line, and I would flatten the ratio.

### My attack on her table

Eleanor's Apple model steps revenue growth down to **4%** for FY2026–FY2030. Her own five
trailing periods run roughly **15% down to 8%**.

My attack is not that 4% is too low in the abstract — it is that **4% is below the floor of her
own evidence, and nothing in her table derives it.** Her history brackets 8% at the bottom; she
has gone beneath the range she measured and applied the step to every year at once rather than
fading into it. That choice gets counted twice in a five-year DCF: once through the explicit
years, and again through the terminal value, which is computed off the final year's cash flow
and is usually the majority of the answer. A growth assumption set below the historical floor
therefore shrinks both the explicit period and the base the perpetuity is applied to.

**What I would hold instead:** roughly 8% flat — the level her trailing periods actually settled
at. If she wants 4%, the burden is to name what changes in Apple's business between now and
FY2030 that her five periods of history do not already contain.

## Reflect

**The label I would defend the longest:** the R&D ratio — which is also the one Eleanor attacked,
and the answer is above. It is the only judgment in my table that contradicts its own history,
which means it is the only one that cannot be defended by pointing at the past. It has to be
defended by an argument about *why the past stops applying*, and that is a harder and more
honest thing to have to do.

**The number in the filing that surprised me:** inventory days at **125**. I had NVIDIA filed
mentally as an asset-light designer, and 125 days is dealership territory — ABG's own number is
in the same range. It is the clearest evidence in the statements that "fabless" does not mean
"inventory-light": NVIDIA has pushed manufacturing out and pulled the inventory risk back in,
which is the same fact the supply-obligation line measures from the liability side.

## Limitations

- **The organic share of FY2026 growth is `unresolved`.** NVIDIA discloses no
  owned-versus-acquired split, and FY2026 was a material acquisition year.
- **The surplus distribution is assumed, not observed.** NVIDIA is accumulating cash; the model
  assumes it is returned. Stated above rather than smoothed.
- **Beta carries the answer.** The $48–$74 grid moves almost entirely on one input, and
  `beta.py` shows that input ranges from 1.815 to 2.216 across defensible specifications.
- **The terminal value is 59.0% of the equity value**, so more than half of what this model says
  NVIDIA is worth rests on a perpetuity formula applied to FY2031.
- **No segment build.** The model runs consolidated; Compute & Networking is 89.6% of revenue,
  so a segment build would mostly re-derive the consolidated answer, but it would separate the
  Graphics decline from the data-centre growth.
